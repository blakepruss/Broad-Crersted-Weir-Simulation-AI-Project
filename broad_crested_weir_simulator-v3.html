What is a broad-crested weir?

A broad-crested weir is a raised obstruction across an open channel with a relatively long, flat top. Water flows over the crest, and under suitable conditions the flow becomes critical on the crest — making it a reliable flow-measurement device.

How does it work?
  1. Water approaches the weir.
    The obstruction raises the upstream level. Head H = y₁ − P above the crest drives the flow. Upstream flow is subcritical (Fr₁ < 1).
  2. Water accelerates onto the crest.
    As water rises over the crest its depth decreases and velocity increases. Specific energy is exchanged between depth and velocity.
  3. Critical flow develops on the crest.
    The crest acts as a hydraulic control. The Froude number reaches 1 — flow velocity equals the shallow-water wave speed √(g y,). This is why discharge can be calculated from the upstream head alone.
  4. Flow accelerates downstream.
    Water becomes supercritical (Fr > 1) on the downstream face. A hydraulic jump may form further downstream depending on tailwater conditions.
Froude number

Fr < 1: subcritical (tranquil).   Fr = 1: critical.   Fr > 1: supercritical (rapid).

The broad-crested weir forces Fr = 1 at the crest, decoupling the upstream measurement from downstream disturbances — the key property that makes it a reliable flow gauge.

Engineering applications
  • Open-channel flow measurement.
    The primary engineering use. Because critical flow establishes on the crest, discharge is calculated from the upstream head alone. Standards include BS EN ISO 4374 and ISO 3846.
  • Irrigation and water distribution.
    Broad-crested weirs measure and regulate water delivery through agricultural canals. Their robust, flat crest tolerates debris and sediment better than sharp-crested alternatives.
  • Wastewater and stormwater management.
    Used in treatment-plant channels and stormwater outfalls to monitor flow rates and control hydraulic grade lines.
  • River gauging and flood hydrology.
    Permanent gauging structures in rivers provide long-term flow records for flood-frequency analysis and water-resource planning.
  • Hydraulics teaching laboratories.
    Demonstrate specific energy, critical depth, the Froude number, and hydraulic jumps in a controlled flume setting.
Broad-crested vs. sharp-crested weirs

A sharp-crested (thin-plate) weir is more accurate at low heads but is fragile and sensitive to crest damage. A broad-crested weir is more robust, handles higher sediment loads, and is preferred for permanent field installations. Typical C𝐡 values range from 0.848 to 0.99 depending on crest geometry and approach conditions (ISO 4374).

When is submergence a concern?

Free-flow conditions require the tailwater to remain below the critical depth on the crest (y₂ < P + y,). When tailwater rises above this level the crest is submerged and the free-flow equation overestimates discharge. A submergence correction factor (Villemonte or similar) must then be applied.
